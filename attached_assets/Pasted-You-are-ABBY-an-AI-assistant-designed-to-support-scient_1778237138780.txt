In the previous module with have seen, that the classical structure of scientific papers is the IMRaD structure. IMRaD stands for introduction, material and methods, results, and discussion. We've also seen that this structure tells a story. The story starts with an opening on our research topic and the niche, which is the more specific domain of research. The opening leads to a challenge, our research problem. Then we bring a solution to this challenge, namely the research we have performed. Our research is the action of our story, which is described at the end of the introduction and culminates with our results, the climax of our story. And finally, at the end of our paper, we bring a resolution to our story with the discussion, which closes all the doors that we have opened so far so that our readers understand in which way our research has solved the problem that we have described in the introduction and in which way it enlightens our research topic.
You may have noticed that I have represented this structure in the shape of an hourglass. Do you have any idea why I use this shape?
The reason why I used an hourglass is not that time is ticking and you should quickly write the damn paper. Maybe time is ticking and very likely you should write the damn paper, but I use this shape to illustrate the fact that your paper should start broadly, then narrow down, and then broaden again.
Indeed in the introduction, we start with our broad research topic. In the example I used in the previous video, we started with breast cancer and explained that breast cancer is one of the most deadly diseases for women. Then we narrow the introduction down to the niche, namely the diagnosis of breast cancer, with an emphasis on mammograms. Then the introduction becomes even more specific when we describe the problem: “ Many women don’t undergo mammograms because it requires effort and it is an uncomfortable procedure.” Now we tackle a smaller problem within the big field of research on mammograms. Finally, we arrive at the central point of our paper: the solution that we bring, which is the the research that we performed. In the fictive example I took it was the development and testing of a new diagnosis method: portable mammograms.
We have arrived to the neck of the hourglass: The material and methods and the result section. These two sections are very specific. We provide a whole bunch of details to describe our research. The fact that it is so specific explain why for many scientists these two sections are the easiest to write.
As I told you in the previous module, the discussion entails a certain symmetry with the introduction. We start narrowly by recapping the problem and the main findings. Then, we broaden the discussion by interpreting the results within the niche. Finally, we open the discussion to future studies and wrap it up with a broad conclusion that comes back to where we started the paper, namely the broad research topic.
It is useful to think about the symmetry between your introduction and your discussion to make your paper understandable and leave your readers satisfied. The introduction creates an expectation in the readers and the discussion is the response to this expectation. If the opening is broader than the conclusion, the reader will have the feeling that the article didn’t deliver what it promised and will be disappointed. Conversely, if the opening is narrower than the conclusion, the reader might not understand the logic that led to this conclusion and end up feeling confused. Matching the opening and conclusion satisfies the expectations of the reader and provides a feeling of closure to the paper.
How broad should your opening be? In general, it is wise to aim for a broad opening, because the broader the opening, the more readers may be interested, and the more citations your article will get. However, the research should also satisfy the expectations that the opening triggers. Articles that overstate the importance of their contribution make disappointed readers (and reviewers). You should thus frame the opening as broadly as possible, given the conclusions that you can draw from the research.
The breadth of your opening will also depend on your audience. If you’re writing your paper for a general audience, it is better to start your paper broadly, by informing them for example that breast cancer has high mortality. If you address specialists, you can be more specific from the beginning on and, in our example directly start with the topic of mammogram. That’s the reason why I asked in the prewriting workbook to try and identify your audience.
The story structure that I have described so far is called the OCAR structure, the letters standing for opening, challenge, action, climax, and resolution. It is the classical structure of many books, tales, the three little pigs, for example, or movies, such as Harry Potter and of most scientific articles. It’s the simplest and most logical structure as each part leads to the next. But there are other structures.
Another kind of story structure that has become very popular over the last years is the ABDCE structure. ABDCE stands for Action, background, development, climax, and ending. The ABDCE structure is similar to the OCAR structure with the perk that the story begins with an intense scene that throws you immediately into the action.
Many James bond movies follow this structure. For example, the opening scene of Spectre shows Daniel Craig tracking down an assassin in Mexico City during the Day of the Deads parade. The scene is dramatic and grabs immediately the audience's attention. Only after this scene does the movie reveals what has motivated this action.
This kind of structure is from a narrative perspective more complex than the OCAR structure. I don’t know for you but I usually struggle to understand the stories in James Bond movies. But the action is so intense that it is nevertheless compelling. The ABDCE structure aims at impatient audiences who may give up on the story if it doesn’t hook them immediately.
Scientific papers sometimes also follow this structure. The text then start with a short summary of the methods and results or with an example, a short story, or a quote that creates an emotion in the reader.
Here is an example of one of my favourite scientific writers, Jonathan Haidt. He’s a psychologist who work on moral judgment and this paper starts with the provocative story of Julie and Mark, who are brother and sister and decide for one night to make love with each other. He uses this example to show that moral intuitions are driven by emotions rather than reason and to grab the attention of his readers from the first sentences on. I’m not going to tell more about this paper because it doesn’t matter here but if you want to see more concretely how Haidt uses the ABDCE structure in his paper, you can find a link to the paper in the resources of this module.
You can find the ABDCE structure in some scientific articles, theses (PhD, master, bachelor theses) as well as in grant proposals. Often grant proposals start with a short description of the most exciting idea the grant aims to demonstrate and then the more classical background description starting with the research topic, the niche, the problem, etc. etc.
A third type of story structure is the LDR structure. LDR stands for Lead, Development, and Resolution. The lead describes the core of the story in a short section, that encloses the opening, the challenge and the solution to the challenge. This section can be as short as one sentence. The middle trunk of the story is its development, and a few sentences wrap up the resolution.
The LDR structure is used by journalists who need the lead to quickly grasp the attention of readers and convey their message efficiently. In the academic world, you will find this kind of structure in high impact journals, such as Nature or Science, who offer short format articles to a general audience. These receive tons of submissions and they don’t have the time to read all these papers. Thus, if you want to publish in one of these journals you need a lead strong enough to motivate the editors to read the whole paper and send it out for review. Think also about including good illustrations of your methods and results that will make the paper visually appealing and communicate in short time the main message. If you try your luck in one of these journals, be prepared though to get a desk rejection. A desk rejection means that the editorial team reject your paper without even sending out to reviewers.
I sent once a paper to Science. I remember it was a Friday at the beginning of the afternoon when I pressed the submit button and I felt so relieved to have this paper off my to-do list. I just lingered a bit longer at the office taking care of minor tasks, when two hours later I received the desk rejection. Needless to say that it didn’t make my weekend brighter.
Here is an illustrious example of a Nature article published in 1953 following the LDR structure: Molecular structure of nucleic acids and it is authored by Watson and Crick. That’s the paper in which they published their discovery of DNA double helix.
And here reads the first sentence, the lead: “We wish to suggest a structure for the salt of deoxyribose nucleic acid (D.N.A.). This structure has novel features which are of considerable biological interest.” You see that the lead goes right to the point, directly shedding the light on the character (the structure of DNA) and its importance. The rest of the paper, which is amazingly short, provides the arguments for the double helix and concludes in one sentence.

In this video, we have explored three different ways to structure stories in general and in scientific papers: The OCAR structure, the ABDCE structure, and the LDR structure. All of these structures incorporate the five story elements (opening, challenge, action, climax, and resolution) although each of them does it in its own way: The OCAR structure is for patient readers, who are willing to let the story unfold naturally, the ABDCE structure is for less patient readers who need a taste of action before getting into the story, and the LDR structure is for the less patient readers of all as it fasts forward the message in the most condensed form.
You can recognize these structures by looking at the introduction: Introductions of OCAR papers are quite long and progressive. Introduction of ABDCE structures start with a summary of the hypotheses, or an example, or a quote, or a short story, whereas introductions of LDR papers are very short, usually 3 paragraphs long.
These three structures are probably the most frequent ones but they are not the only ones. Certain fields of research or certain journals may require a different structure.
For example the famous journal PNAS requests that authors report the Material and Methods at the end of the paper, after the discussion and before the references. Other journals can have other specific requirements, such as asking for a section describing the theoretical background. There is a diversity of structures for publications in journals. And the same is true for other scientific texts. For example, if you write a grant proposal, the agency to which you are applying will probably also request certain information that will dictate how you structure your proposal. Whether you write an article, a grant proposal, a thesis, or something else, it is essential to adapt the structure of your paper to the outlet.
On this slide is the example of a grant proposal I submitted to the Austrian Science Foundation. The guidelines requested a description of the Scientific aspects and of the human resources. Concerning the scientific aspects, they mentioned that reviewers would evaluate five aspects: the relevance to international research in the field, the project, the objectives and hypotheses, the scientific originality and innovation, as well as the methods. They didn’t explicitly asked to structure the proposal accordingly, but I nevertheless did it: I dedicated one section for each of these aspects. 
Whether you write a grant proposal, a thesis, or an article, you want to make the task of the reader as easy as possible and you can use the structure for that. If you know in advance that they will evaluate a certain dimension of your paper, make it salient, so that they will know where to look for it and give them the answer to the questions that you know they will have to address: If they judge the originality of your ideas, create a section entitled scientific originality and write in it: ”A major innovation of this project is this and that.” When the reviewers will look for this information they will find it easily and have good arguments to support your project.

So far we’ve see that the essence of the paper lays in its story. We’ve also zoomed a bit more in the details of the structure of a paper and its different sections. Now we’re going to zoom in even further by looking at paragraph structure.
Paragraphs are the bone structure, the skeleton of our paper. When we look at a paper, this paragraph skeleton is the first thing that we see, our first visual impression of the paper. But paragraphs are more than a visual input. They constitute the structure through which the thread of your story unfolds.
How to leverage this structure to write an outline that unfolds a logical thread
Paragraphs support the story of our paper like scenes do in a movie. In a movie, each scene tells a little story that leads to the next scene and so does the general story unfold. In a movie each scene revolves around a central theme. For example, in Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire, there is the scene in which the students from Durmstrang and Beauxbatons, the two foreign wizard schools, arrive at Hogwarts. Then there is a scene in which Dumbledore reveals the contestants of the Triwizard tournament, another one in which Harry fights the dragon etc. So you see each scene has one main purpose that you could summarise in just one sentence. The same is true with paragraphs in a scientific article.
Each of your paragraphs of your paper should develop one main idea. I’m going to repeat this information because it is important: Each paragraphs should develop one and only one main idea.
You can think about the number of paragraphs you have, for example, in your introduction as the number of ideas you can develop in the introduction.
This notion is very useful for your writing process. I already told you that I find that there is nothing more wasteful in time and energy than writing a first draft of your paper to discover that it is way too long and that you have to make major cuts. Now you can shorten a paper a little bit. You can remove a sentence here and there or delete words or references that are not essential. This is feasible. But drastically shortening a paper is difficult, having to remove whole paragraphs is almost impossible because if your paper is well structured the first paragraph leads to the second, which leads to the third, which leads to the fourth etc. In other words, the first idea, in the first paragraph, leads logically to the second idea, which leads logically to the third one etc. In the same way as you cannot remove a whole scene in a movie without altering the story and potentially making it incomprehensible, you cannot delete a whole paragraph in your paper and keep your narration intact.
In this course, I would like to offer you to make use of this notion when creating your outline. In particular, I would like to invite you to count the number of paragraphs that you will have at your disposal, especially in the introduction and in the discussion.
If you know that you have between 5 and 7 paragraphs to introduce your research, then you know that you will have 5 to 7 ideas to make the points that we saw in the previous module. You will have one paragraph to explain what your research topic is and why it’s interesting for your readers, one or two paragraphs to describe your niche, one or two paragraphs for your problem, another one for introducing your hypotheses, and a last one for your methods.
Now if the articles published in the journal where you intend to submit your paper usually have very long introductions, let’s say between 12 and 15 paragraphs, you will have to adapt your outline accordingly. In that case, the description of the niche won’t be two paragraphs long but four or five and the ideas that you will developed in these paragraphs will be more specific.
If on the contrary, you want to write a very short format paper and you may have to make all these points in one paragraph, using one or two sentences per point.
Formats and lengths of article differ a lot from journal to journal. During this course I will ask you to count the number of paragraphs in the introductions and in the discussions of your sample papers and to use this information to create an outline that fits the journal.
Paragraphs make for the structure of the different sections of your paper and when we open an article, that’s the first thing that we see: the sections and the paragraphs! Paragraphs also have their own structure.
To make your articles more understandable, it can be useful to structure your paragraphs in the following way.
Start your paragraphs with a topic sentence that states the main idea of the paragraph. The topic sentence is followed by the body of the paragraph. In the body you may find explanations or definitions, especially in the introduction it is often useful to define our concepts. The body often also includes evidence: Facts, examples, experiments, studies, or results that support the main idea of the paragraph. And the body also includes comments from the authors: Comments that explain what the evidence mean and how it relates to the point. Comments that consist in comparing opposing evidence or that raise implications or limitations.
Finally, to bring closure to your paragraph, it is useful to finish it with a concluding sentence. There are different kinds of concluding sentences. You can finish the paragraph by outlining its implications. Or you can show the development an argument. That can be useful if in the paragraph you have described a complex process, such as a long chain reaction or a cycle and you want to finish the paragraph by recapping the main steps. Or you can use your concluding sentence for a third purpose, which I personally love, which is to link to the main idea of the next paragraph. I love this last method because it creates a little cliffhanger at the end of the paragraph that motivates the reader to read the next paragraph.
This structure of paragraphs is called point-first paragraphs because you first make your point and then you provide the explanation and evidence.
I’m going to show you an concrete example of this paragraph structure: The first paragraph of the introduction of one of my papers.
The first sentence reads:
“Among all the qualities one may wish to develop, the ability to control one’s behavior when exposed to temptations is the most useful one.”
That’s the topic sentence. You remember that I advised you to start your introduction by explaining why your research topic is interesting. That’s exactly what I did here. The research topic is (can you guess?) self-control phrased here as the ability to control one’s behavior when exposed to temptations. And this paragraph aims at explaining why self-control is important.
The topic sentence makes a general statement, which is then supported the next sentences.
“Psychological research has demonstrated that people who are able to control themselves obtain better academic grades, encounter greater career successes, and reach a higher socioeconomic status. Self-control is also related to better social skills, less violent behaviors, healthier habits, and a lower risk of developing psychopathology, obesity, or addictions. Promoting self-control is thus a crucial challenge for parents, educators, politicians, and individuals who want to develop self-discipline.”
To make reading this paragraph easier, I have removed all references but they are included in the original paper.
The first two sentences composing the body, which is written in orange on the slide, report scientific evidence that support the topic sentence. And then I comment on this evidence, by mentioning that:
“Promoting self-control is thus a crucial challenge for parents, educators, politicians, and individuals who want to develop self-discipline.”
In this example, there is no definition but I could also have provided a definition of self-control. I didn’t do it because I knew that my audience already knew what self-control is.
And then we have the concluding sentence. So far in the paragraph, I have explained that the world needs ways to improve self-control and finish by pointing to the fact that:
“Social learning theory suggests that observing others who master self-control represents an efficient strategy for this purpose.”
That’s the cliffhanger, the transition to the next paragraph.
If you’re not American, this paragraph structure, i.e., point-first paragraphs, may be quite different from what you have learned at school. In France, where I grew up, I have learned that you should first present the evidence and then derive the general conclusion. In a way, here we do the opposite: We start with the conclusion and then we provide the evidence. It may seem illogical but this is the most compelling way to communicate.

Point-first paragraphs are useful to both read papers and write them.
Indeed, you can use this paragraph structure to fast read papers. Often you don’t need to read a paper from A to Z. If you’re looking for a certain information or just want to have an overview of let’s say the introduction, then you can read the first sentence of each paragraph and you will know what the paragraph is about. You will be able to figure the narration of the introduction without having to read everything. That can save you a lot of time.
At the level of writing, you can use this structure to write your outline. You can do that in two ways:
* Either you write the topic sentence of each paragraph, then you’ll know which general idea you will develop in the paragraph.
* Or you write the topic and concluding sentences of the paragraph. The advantage of writing also the concluding sentence is that it forces you to consider the transitions between paragraphs and fine tune the thread before spending too much time working on the body of each paragraph. This method is what I call writing the skeleton of your paper.
You first identify your narration by outlining the topic and concluding sentences of your paragraphs and only when you feel that you have a nice thread you invest time in crafting each paragraph.
Some scientific writers love this method, they feel that it saves a lot of time and brings clarity to their structure. But you have to test it for yourself. Like every domain, in scientific writing there is no one size fits all, and you need to experiment a bit to find out what works for you.
In any case, whether you create your outline by just writing the topic sentence or writing the topic and concluding sentences, I would advise you to write full sentences with a subject and a verb instead of keywords.
Many scientists write an outline by making a list of keywords to figure out how they will structure their paper. Something like what you can see on this slide. In my experience this method can lead to problems, later, at the drafting level.
Indeed, at the moment when one writes the keywords it feels clear and logical. But the problem with keywords is that they are vague. For example, if I write in my outline “Literature on self-control”, what do I mean? There is a lot of literature on self-control. What is the idea that I want to extract from this literature and expose in my paper?
Writing an actual sentence that represents the idea of the paragraph, i.e., writing the topic sentence of the paragraph forces you to be much more precise. In the self-control example, that sentence could be:
“Self-control is an essential skill for every day life.”
or “Many people lack self-control.”
or “The world is full of temptations.”
All these sentences could fall within the general term of literature on self-control and all three could be used to open the introduction of a paper on that topic. But you can see that they express different ideas and are associated with different evidence and arguments. They also give a different tone to the paper.
All that to advice you when you write your outline to rely on sentences (with a subject and verb at least) instead of keywords.

As a kid, I used to be terrified of the wolf. As soon as he entered the story, I would just stand up with great concentration next to the record player.

The wolf fell into the boiling water.”
The climax scene is the scene in a story where everything falls into place. In an action movie, the climax scene is the last fight in which the hero confronts the villain; in a romantic comedy, it is the scene where the guy tries to catch the girl before she takes off on a plane because she is so disappointed that their relationship didn't work out; in a detective story, it will be the scene where the murderer is revealed. It's an intense scene.
In a children's story, identifying the climax scene is easy: it's when they start to freak out. I would start screaming every time the wolf climbed up the chimney. The resolution concludes the story and brings closure to the audience. The resolution closes all the doors that were opened during the story. The little pigs had been given a mission by their mom to make their own lives and build a house to protect them. Now they have killed the wolf, they have learned that they should not be lazy and that they should pursue their goals even if when it becomes difficult. All is well!
The resolution helps cooling down after the climax scene and brings closure to the story so that we can let go of it and resume our lives.

Here I want to insist on the fact that describing the problem that your research will address before presenting a solution makes your contribution bigger and will make it more interesting for your readers. In the same way as in a movie the challenge that the hero faces creates a tension in the audience that motivates them to keep on watching, in a scientific article if you start by describing a problem you create tension in your readers and motivate them to keep on reading because they will only feel better after knowing that there is a solution to this problem. Your research topic is also not a description of your hypotheses such as “We predict that the amygdala reacts to fearful faces.” And it’s not your main result either “T cells of the immune system develop in three steps”.

Most of the scientists I work with are very aware of the details of their research. They see the trees but not the forest. But to find your research topic you need to take some distance and have a broader view. This is something that’s difficult at first but here are a few questions that can help you find out what’s your research topic.
The first one is : What are the implications of your research? Some research projects have direct applications, such as improving the treatment of a disease. In those cases, finding your research topic is easy, the disease or its treatment is your topic. In other cases, the applications are less obvious. Then you can either consider the long-term applications. Maybe your results won’t immediately be useful but by contributing to the scientific knowledge, they may in the middle or long-term be beneficial for something, this something being your research topic. You can also ask yourself why your readers should care about your research. Let’s say that you’re working on the evolution across species of a certain bone. Understanding how that bone has evolved across mammals won’t immediately save the world but it may increase our knowledge of the origins of mankind, which has triggered human curiosity for millennia. In this example, the origin of mankind could be your research topic.
A third possibility to find out what your research topic is consists in identifying your independent and dependent variables. In an experimental design, the independent variable is the variable that you manipulate and the dependent variable the variable that you measure. The dependent variable is usually your research topic. For example, if I run an experiment in which I’m testing whether spending time in nature helps people relax, spending time in nature is my independent variable, and being relaxed is my dependent variable as well as my research topic. In this example, the finality of my research is to offer people ways to relax.

When people have already written an abstract for their research, they try to force the sentences they already have into these questions. I suspect that more or less unconsciously they don’t want to expand the effort of writing something new. And of course, every abstract is to a certain extent similar to this structure.
But usually, the abstract that they have already written miss some points: Usually, it doesn’t start by explaining why the research topic is important and it doesn’t state the problem although these are critical elements. The opening and the challenge give the flavor, the direction, the thread of your research. You need them in your abstract. Even if you’re very limited in your number of words. You need to have these ingredients in.
I’m asking to put on the side what you have already written and answer the workbook questions as they are. This will make a world of difference in your abstract.
